Policy & Regulatory Solutions (6)

Enacted laws, permits, and proposed rules that require or incentivize water-responsible data center siting, design, and reporting.

Deployed
Virginia Monthly DC Water Reporting Mandate
State: Virginia General Assembly / Dept. of Environmental Quality · Source
HB 496 / SB 553 (enacted April 2026) requires water utilities to report monthly aggregate volumes delivered to data centers to the State Water Control Board. Creates the first mandatory utility-level data stream for DC water use in the US.
Loudoun Water (serving ~1.635B gal/yr to data centers) must file monthly reports beginning ~July 2026. First published data will be the only facility-aggregated DC water series in any US state.
→ VA HB 496 / SB 553 tracked in Legislation tab
Deployed
Idaho Closed-Loop Cooling Mandate
State: Idaho Legislature
H895 (enacted 2026) requires new data centers to use closed-loop or non-consumptive cooling systems as a permit condition, eliminating the largest evaporative water loss pathway. Designed to protect the Snake River Plain aquifer.
First state-level mandate of its kind in the US. South Carolina HB 4583 (pending) would extend this closed-loop model to the Southeast.
→ The two enacted closed-loop mandates — SC HB 4583 · ID H 895
Deployed
South Dakota Cost-Causation Billing
State: South Dakota Legislature
SB 135 (enacted 2026) requires data centers ≥10 MW to demonstrate water-supply compatibility and mandates that developers bear the marginal cost of supply-infrastructure expansions they trigger — not residential ratepayers.
Part of South Dakota's 'Data Center Bill of Rights.' Addresses the pattern where large industrial loads trigger regional water infrastructure upgrades whose costs are socialized to all ratepayers.
→ SD SB 135 in Legislation tab
Deployed
Utah Water Disclosure Law + Executive Order
State: Utah Legislature / Governor Spencer Cox
HB 76 (enacted March 2026) mandates projected and actual water consumption disclosure from facilities ≥10,000 sq ft. EO 2026-03 (May 2026) directs agencies to require water-impact review before approvals and evaluate cost-attribution models.
Disclosure threshold set to match existing utility metering capabilities, making reporting immediately actionable rather than dependent on new infrastructure.
→ UT HB 76 and UT EO 2026-03 in Legislation tab
Pilot / In Progress
Ohio EPA Data Center Wastewater General Permit (OHD000001)
Federal: Ohio EPA (US EPA Region 5) · Source
First proposed general permit requiring data centers to file Discharge Monitoring Reports (DMRs) directly for cooling-tower blowdown, closing the gap where DC cooling water routes through municipal WWTPs with no facility-level accounting.
Comment period closed January 2025; permit finalization pending as of June 2026. If enacted, it would create the first direct federal-level DC water monitoring dataset, complementing the WWTP-level data this project already tracks via EPA ECHO.
→ The draft general permit itself; discharge data will come from the oh_epa_general_permit scraper once it is finalized — OH EPA OHD000001 (draft general permit)
Proposed
New York SEQRA Environmental Review for 50MW+ Facilities
State: New York Legislature (awaiting governor signature) · Source
S10642 / A11560 requires SEQRA environmental-impact assessment and public comment before any data center project exceeds 50 MW, including a mandatory water-supply analysis. Would be the broadest state environmental-review trigger for data centers in the US.
Passed Senate 44-16 and Assembly 102-39 as of June 2026. Gov. Hochul has not publicly committed to signing or vetoing.
→ NY S10642 / A11560 in Legislation tab
Utility & Infrastructure Solutions (5)

Water utility programs and regional planning approaches that integrate data center demand into supply management, capacity planning, and dual-feed infrastructure.

Deployed
Dual-Feed Systems: Potable + Reclaimed Water Connections
Utility: Loudoun Water (Virginia) · Source
Data centers receive two separate connections — potable for human-contact uses and reclaimed (recycled, non-potable) water for cooling-tower makeup — reducing pressure on drinking water supplies.
Loudoun Water ACFR 2023: 899M gal potable + 736M gal reclaimed delivered to data centers annually. Reclaimed water is 45% of total DC supply — the highest reclaimed fraction of any US data center utility cluster tracked here.
→ Loudoun Water ACFR data tracked in Sources tab
Deployed
ERU-Based Demand Planning via Industrial User Surveys
Utility: Prince William Water (Virginia) · Source
Annual Industrial User Surveys allocate Equivalent Residential Units (ERUs) to data center customers, giving utility planners binding demand commitments for capital planning before construction begins.
2024 survey: 56 data centers hold 3,276 ERUs = ~478M gal/yr (avg 58.5 ERU per facility). ERU allocations are reviewed with each expansion permit, creating a cumulative demand ledger that's used to size infrastructure upgrades.
→ Prince William Water IUS data in Sources tab
Deployed
Aggregate DC Water Metering and Annual Public Reporting
Utility: Loudoun Water, Fairfax Water (Virginia) · Source
Utilities publish aggregate data center water deliveries in annual financial reports (ACFRs), providing the only publicly available utility-level DC water volumes in Virginia — currently the primary Tier 1 data source for this tracker.
Loudoun Water ACFR 2023: 1.635B gal to data centers (~15% of total utility sales). Fairfax Water supplies ~18 MGD wholesale to Loudoun. Virginia HB 496 will formalize and extend this to monthly reports.
→ Loudoun Water and Fairfax Water data in Sources tab
Pilot / In Progress
Regional Demand Forecasting with Data Center Growth Scenarios
Utility: Central Ohio Regional Water Authority / Ohio EPA · Source
Collaborative regional water demand study incorporating data center growth scenarios to enable proactive infrastructure investment rather than reactive expansion after capacity constraints appear.
March 2025 study projects industrial demand: ~10 MGD (2024) → 40 MGD (2030) → 90 MGD (2050). Intel New Albany campus alone: ~6 MGD. Study is informing $2B+ in regional water infrastructure planning.
→ Central Ohio projections in Sources tab
Pilot / In Progress
AWS / Greater Western Water Recycled-Water Connection (Melbourne)
Utility: Greater Western Water (Victoria, Australia) / Amazon Web Services · Source
Greater Western Water signed an agreement to supply AWS's planned Melbourne-area data center with recycled water from its Melton Recycled Water Plant at Mt Cottrell — the first such connection for a data center in the state of Victoria, announced mid-June 2026.
First-of-its-kind government-utility-brokered recycled-water supply contract specifically for a data center in Victoria, Australia, intended to remove cooling-tower demand from the potable supply for the under-development facility.
Technology & Operational Solutions (7)

Engineering approaches that reduce or eliminate water consumption through improved cooling design, alternative water sources, and efficiency metrics.

Deployed
Closed-Loop Recirculating Cooling Towers
Industry: Hyperscalers (AWS, Google, Microsoft, Meta) — industry standard
Recirculating systems reuse the same water in a closed loop, dramatically reducing freshwater withdrawal compared to once-through systems. This is the current standard for all modern hyperscale facilities and the technology basis for Idaho's closed-loop mandate.
Typical WUE (Water Usage Effectiveness): 1.5–2.0 L/kWh for recirculating towers vs. 3–4 L/kWh for older designs. Industry target: WUE < 1.2 L/kWh. Still consumptive (evaporation) but far more efficient than alternatives.
Deployed
Reclaimed / Recycled Water Sourcing
Industry: Google (Prineville OR), Meta, Loudoun Water DC customers
Data centers contract with utilities or municipalities for reclaimed (non-potable recycled) water to supply cooling towers, freeing potable water for human use. Requires dual-plumbing infrastructure but is well-established in water-stressed regions.
Loudoun Water: 736M gal/yr reclaimed delivered to data centers (45% of total DC supply). Google's Prineville, OR campus uses 100% reclaimed water for cooling. Reclaimed water WUE is identical to potable — the gain is purely in source substitution.
→ Loudoun Water reclaimed volumes in Sources tab
Deployed
Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE) Self-Reporting
Industry: Green Grid (standard-setter), Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon
WUE (liters of water per kilowatt-hour of IT load) is an industry self-reporting metric enabling year-over-year comparison. Analogous to PUE for power efficiency, it allows tracking progress without facility-level disclosure — though no independent verification exists.
Google 2024: WUE 1.10 L/kWh (global average). Microsoft FY2023: WUE 0.33 L/kWh (includes 'net-water replenishments' — accounting treatment disputed). Meta: 0.26 L/kWh. No independent auditor verifies these figures — see Company Claims tab for verbatim operator quotes.
→ Company Claims in Legislation tab (Company Water Claims section)
Pilot / In Progress
Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) for AI / GPU Clusters
Industry: Google, Microsoft, Meta (AI training facilities)
Liquid coolant is circulated directly to processors and server components, removing heat without relying on large evaporative cooling towers. Near-zero cooling water required for DLC-equipped racks — the cooling burden shifts to heat exchangers that can reject to ambient air.
Adoption accelerating for AI training clusters (H100/H200/GB200 GPU pods) where heat density exceeds air-side cooling limits. Microsoft reports WUE near 0 for full-DLC racks. At scale, eliminates the cooling-tower evaporation entirely.
Pilot / In Progress
Air-Side / Dry Cooling (Zero Evaporation)
Industry: Microsoft (Dublin, Ireland; Helsinki, Finland), UK operators
In cooler climates, outside air rejects heat without any water evaporation — 'free cooling' or 'economizer mode.' Zero consumptive water use when operating in full air-side mode. Feasibility depends on wet-bulb temperature and local climate.
Microsoft's Dublin data center uses air-side cooling 95%+ of the year, achieving near-zero WUE. Not viable for Virginia summer conditions without backup wet cooling; becomes more viable for data centers sited in northern latitudes or at altitude.
Pilot / In Progress
Nvidia Vera Rubin DSX Reference Design (Near-Zero Water Cooling)
Industry: Nvidia, with Motivair (Schneider Electric's advanced cooling division) · Source
Nvidia unveiled the Vera Rubin DSX data center reference design at London Climate Week (June 2026): a fully liquid-cooled architecture using a 75% water / 25% propylene glycol coolant circulated in a closed loop at up to 45°C, paired with outdoor dry coolers that reject heat to ambient air in favorable climates, eliminating on-site water consumption for cooling.
Nvidia estimates the design cuts water use from the ~2.6 million gallons per MW/year typical of cooling-tower systems to near zero, saving a 50 MW hyperscale facility over $4M/year in combined cooling energy and water costs. It is a reference design for the upcoming Rubin GPU platform; no named customer deployment has been announced yet.
Proposed
Immersion Cooling (Full Liquid Submersion)
Industry: Submer, Wiwynn, GRC, early Microsoft pilots
Servers are fully submerged in dielectric (non-conductive) fluid that absorbs heat directly. The fluid is cooled via compact heat exchangers that can reject to ambient air with minimal or zero evaporative water loss — potentially eliminating cooling water entirely at scale.
Commercial deployments remain rare at hyperscale as of 2026, primarily in HPC and crypto mining. Microsoft and others have published pilot results. Long-term potential to reduce WUE to ~0 without requiring a cold climate or DLC-compatible processor packages.
