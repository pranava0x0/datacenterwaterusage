What can go wrong

cyber

Internet-exposed PLC compromise

IRGC-affiliated CyberAv3ngers compromised Unitronics PLCs used by US water and wastewater systems, focusing on internet-exposed devices with default or absent passwords. Investigators observed operational manipulation, including deleted ladder logic.

Operational meaning: One exposed controller can bridge an internet intrusion into pumps, valves, alarms, or chemical processes.

Sources: CISA/FBI/EPA advisory AA23-335A
hybrid

Insider and former-contractor access

A former instrumentation and control technician was federally charged after allegedly retaining remote access to Discovery Bay, California, uninstalling the main operational and monitoring system, and shutting down supporting servers.

Operational meaning: Offboarding, named accounts, privilege limits, and monitored remote access are operational controls, not paperwork.

Sources: US Attorney, Northern District of California
cyber

Ransomware and IT-to-OT propagation

GAO found that attacks have disrupted water operations and that legacy equipment, workforce gaps, and limited voluntary investment constrain defenses where business IT connects to operational technology.

Operational meaning: Segmentation, tested recovery, and safe manual operation matter as much as detecting malware.

Sources: GAO-24-106744 · GAO-26-109159
physical

Physical intrusion and intentional contamination

Credible physical pathways include breached fences and hatches, access to finished water, sabotage of remote facilities, and theft or forced release of dangerous treatment chemicals.

Operational meaning: Cameras alone do not close the loop; detection must connect to staffed response, water-quality monitoring, and practiced investigation procedures.

Sources: CISA ChemLock water and wastewater fact sheet · EPA physical security monitoring resources

What defense requires

prevent

Baseline OT hygiene

Remove unnecessary public exposure; inventory IT and OT; replace defaults; use strong authentication; patch or compensate; segment networks; maintain tested backups; and exercise response and recovery.

Limit: Passive monitoring tools do not replace architecture, identity, recovery, or operator practice.

Sources: CISA/EPA/FBI top cyber actions
assess

Utility assessment and mitigation planning

EPA offers free drinking-water, wastewater, and combined-system cybersecurity evaluations covering IT and OT, followed by a gap report and risk-mitigation-plan template.

Limit: Assessment and planning are not implementation funding or continuous defense. EPA says it receives anonymized aggregate results, not utility-specific findings.

Sources: EPA Water Sector Cybersecurity Evaluation Program
prevent

Secure OT remote access

NIST SP 1800-45 gives utilities a replicable reference architecture for remote access to water and wastewater operational technology.

Limit: A reference implementation is not proof that utilities or participating vendors have deployed every control.

Sources: NIST NCCoE SP 1800-45
detect-respond

Physical monitoring and contamination response

Layer facility hardening, door and hatch sensors, alarms, cameras, secure communications, water-quality sensors, complaint surveillance, public-health notification, and practiced investigation procedures.

Limit: Sensors must be commissioned, maintained, and tied to humans with authority to respond.

Sources: EPA water infrastructure resilience research
respond-recover

Threat sharing and mutual aid

WaterISAC distributes sector intelligence; state WARN networks move personnel, equipment, materials, and services between utilities during emergencies.

Limit: These networks do not amount to a uniform national security operations center or mandatory telemetry from every utility.

Sources: WaterISAC · EPA mutual aid and assistance

Operating models worth reusing

EPA/INL Water Security Test Bed

Start with a real cyber-physical proving ground where destructive testing is safe; add representative PLCs, pumps, telemetry, remote access, vendor firmware, and physical intrusion scenarios.

Sources: INL Water Security Test Bed

DOE CyTRICS

Reuse standardized intake, test protocols, vendor agreements, findings formats, component taxonomy, protected analytics, and managed disclosure for a Water-CyTRICS track.

Sources: DOE CyTRICS

LLNL Skyfall and CyTRICS analytics

Pair INL's water loop with hardware-in-the-loop simulation and cross-product software, firmware, and bill-of-materials analysis; never connect research systems to utility production.

Sources: LLNL Skyfall · LLNL CyTRICS work

ORNL Grid Operations and Analytics Laboratory

Use governed operational feeds or replayable derived datasets from willing utilities instead of relying only on synthetic networks.

Sources: ORNL GOAL

EPA evaluation and technical-assistance programs

Use EPA as the front door; preserve utility control of detailed findings and use de-identified aggregates for national prioritization.

Sources: EPA cybersecurity evaluation program

Anthropic Project Glasswing

Begin with a screened cohort, require remediation capacity before testing, coordinate disclosure, measure fixed vulnerabilities rather than findings alone, then expand.

Sources: Project Glasswing

OpenAI Daybreak

Separate routine defensive support from higher-risk validation, deliver through trusted partners and workflows, and subsidize access for public and small critical-infrastructure operators.

Sources: OpenAI Daybreak

Texas Project Watershed 250

A state can convene federal and private capabilities into a no-cost utility pilot, but publish participation, spending, remediation, and outcome measures if the model is to be evaluated.

Sources: Project Watershed 250

Project Confluence

Where water operations, national laboratories, states, and defenders meet.

Mission: Reduce the likelihood and public-health consequences of cyber, physical, supply-chain, and compound attacks on drinking-water and wastewater systems by joining EPA's utility relationships and financing channels with DOE laboratory testing, CISA threat and incident capabilities, state delivery networks, vendor remediation, and utility operations.

Leadership: EPA is accountable lead because water is its sector responsibility. DOE co-leads laboratory testing and cyber-physical research. CISA owns cross-sector threat support and incident coordination, not water regulation.

Governance: Begin with a five-year EPA-DOE interagency agreement and a CISA operating annex. Use the existing Water Government Coordinating Council and Water Sector Coordinating Council for oversight, with a technical steering committee representing states, tribes, utilities of every size and ownership model, WaterISAC, WARNs, public health, labs, vendors, integrators, and independent researchers.

  1. AssessEPA cyber and physical evaluation, asset inventory, external exposure review, dependency mapping, and recovery baseline.
  2. TestWater-CyTRICS component testing, firmware and software composition analysis, hardware-in-the-loop simulation, contamination trials, and patch validation.
  3. ExerciseOperator and executive exercises combining cyber intrusion, physical access, contamination, power and communications loss, manual operation, and WARN mobilization.
  4. FixPrioritized implementation, vendor remediation sprints, segmentation, secure remote access, backup restoration, procurement clauses, and financing support.
  5. ShareProtected WaterISAC advisories, component exposure notices, de-identified trends, state briefings, and public post-remediation lessons.

Regional delivery: Launch four nodes, then scale toward one delivery partnership in every EPA region. Each pairs a state lead, national-lab team, utility or university training site, WaterISAC, and WARN. Nodes bring mobile assessment and test kits, exercises, operator training, vendor remediation clinics, and small-system implementation support.

12 months

Agreement and disclosure rules; four regional nodes; five states and 50 utilities; 25 high-prevalence product families inventoried; 15 tested; 10 combined exercises; first anonymized baseline.

36 months

Delivery presence in every EPA region; 500 utilities served; 100 product families tested; 75% of confirmed high-severity findings mitigated within agreed windows; 2,500 people trained.

60 months

2,000 utilities served; protected national component-exposure map; shorter remediation and restoration times; mature protocols moved into procurement, curricula, vendor practice, and repeatable state programs; independent evaluation before permanent funding.

Measures
  • Days from validated finding to vendor mitigation
  • Days from advisory to utility mitigation or compensating control
  • Critical OT inventory completeness
  • Removal of internet-exposed administration and default credentials
  • Tested backup restoration and safe manual-operation capability
  • Exercise time to detect, isolate, communicate, and restore
  • Product-family coverage and market prevalence
  • Repeat component exposures found across utilities
  • Small, rural, tribal, and disadvantaged-system participation
  • Reduction in repeat assessment findings
Funding paths
  • Dedicated EPA-DOE appropriation for laboratories, regional delivery, mobile equipment, small-system support, and independent evaluation.
  • DWSRF/CWSRF and set-asides for eligible assessments, technical assistance, equipment, workforce, and resilience upgrades; these are eligibility paths, not security-only totals.
  • CISA state/local cyber grants where ownership and eligibility rules permit.
  • DOE CESER and laboratory research funding for reusable testing, analytics, and technology transition.
  • State/local appropriations, rates, bonds, and capital plans for sustained field implementation.
  • Vendor or philanthropic equipment and engineering support with strict conflict controls.
Guardrails
  • Never connect offensive research systems to live water operations.
  • Use coordinated disclosure and emergency exceptions; measure remediation, not raw vulnerability counts.
  • Do not treat voluntary assessments as certifications or publicly rank utilities by vulnerability.
  • Minimize collected data; keep raw captures, credentials, and detailed diagrams with the utility unless it opts in.
  • No participant data for unrelated enforcement, commercial model training, or vendor marketing without separate authority.
  • Require human authorization for AI-assisted exploit validation and every operational change.
  • Give small, rural, tribal, and disadvantaged systems governance seats, not only services.
  • Vendor contributions cannot control test selection, findings, disclosure, or procurement.
